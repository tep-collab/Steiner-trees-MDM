Steiner trees and maximal distance minimizers

A reference on two problems of one-dimensional geometric optimization. Status as of November 2026.

Both problems look for a connected set of minimal length (one-dimensional Hausdorff measure ℋ¹) in Euclidean space ℝd. In the Steiner problem the set must connect a given compact set A. In the maximal distance minimizer problem it must come within distance r of every point of a given compact set M. Locally the solutions look alike: line segments, angles of at least 120°, and triple branching points with angles of exactly 120°.

Steiner problem

The shortest network connecting given points

The (finite) Steiner problem: for a finite set P = {x1, …, xn} ⊂ ℝd find a connected set St(P) of minimal length containing P. A solution is called a Steiner tree, and the points xi are called terminals.

Classical properties

Structure of a solution (see Gilbert–Pollak, 1968; Hwang–Richards–Winter, 1992)
  • A solution exists (not necessarily unique) and is a finite union of segments forming a tree.
  • Every vertex has degree at most 3; only terminals can have degree 1 or 2.
  • The angle between any two adjacent edges is at least 2π/3. At a branching point (a vertex of degree 3) all three angles equal 2π/3 and the three edges lie in one two-dimensional plane, for every d.
  • There are at most n − 2 Steiner points (branching points outside P). If there are exactly n − 2, all terminals have degree 1 and the tree is called full.

A locally minimal tree is a connected acyclic set S ⊃ P that, in a small neighborhood of each of its points, coincides with a Steiner tree for the points on the boundary of the neighborhood. It keeps the local properties above but may be longer than a solution.

The topology of a tree is the abstract graph with labeled terminals and unlabeled Steiner points.

Number of full topologies (Gilbert–Pollak, 1968)

The number of full topologies with n terminals equals (2n − 4)!2n−2 (n − 2)!.

Uniqueness of the realization of a topology (Melzak, 1961; Gilbert–Pollak, 1968; Hwang–Weng, 1992)

For a given topology T and configuration P there is at most one locally minimal tree. For a full topology the length, as a function of the positions of the branching points, is convex and has a unique local minimum.

Constructions and formulas

Melzak reduction (Melzak, 1961)

If a branching point q of a full planar locally minimal tree is adjacent to two pendant terminals p1, p2, they can be replaced by the single vertex p of the equilateral triangle pp1p2 (on the other side from q), with the remaining edge prolonged to p: the length does not change. This yields a ruler-and-compass construction of planar Steiner trees. Turn on “Melzak construction” on the main page.

Maxwell’s formula

For a full locally minimal tree with terminals p1, …, pn ∈ ℂ the length equals Σ c̄kpk, where ck is the unit complex number giving the outer direction of the edge at pk. The length depends only on the terminals and the directions at them; moreover Σ c̄k = 0.

Terminals on a circle (Rubinstein–Thomas, 1992)

Let p1, …, pn lie on a circle of radius r in cyclic order. If at most one of the segments [pipi+1] is longer than r, then a Steiner tree has no Steiner points.

Complexity

NP-hardness (Garey–Graham–Johnson, 1977; Rubinstein–Thomas–Wormald, 1997)

The Euclidean Steiner problem is NP-hard, even when the terminals lie on two lines in the plane.

Dimension ≥ 3 (W. Smith, 1992)

The local structure is the same, but Steiner points are no longer expressible in radicals; there are almost no explicit series of nonplanar solutions. Even for the vertices of a regular simplex in high dimensions the trees are unknown.

Steiner ratio

The Steiner ratio is the infimum, over all finite point sets, of the ratio of the length of a Steiner tree to the length of a minimum spanning tree.

Gilbert–Pollak conjecture (1968)

In the plane the Steiner ratio equals √3/2 ≈ 0.866 and is attained on the vertices of an equilateral triangle. The conjecture is open (Ivanov–Tuzhilin, 2012); the best proven lower bound is about 0.824 (Chung–Graham, 1985).

General bounds (Graham–Hwang, 1976; Smith–Smith, 1995)

In every ℝd the Steiner ratio is at least 1/√3, and in every metric space at least 1/2. The best known examples in ℝd are the “d-sausages”; for d = 3 they give about 0.784.

See also: uniqueness, infinite trees, quantitative regularity.

Uniqueness of the Steiner tree

Ambiguous configurations are rare

A set of n terminals is called ambiguous if it has several Steiner trees. The simplest example is the vertices of a square (see Steiner problem). Configurations of n points in the plane form the space ℝ2n.

The plane

Theorem (Basok–Cherkashin–Rastegaev–Teplitskaya, IMRN 2024) arXiv:1809.01463

For n ≥ 4 the set of ambiguous planar n-point configurations has Hausdorff dimension 2n − 1 as a subset of ℝ2n. In particular it has measure zero: a generic planar configuration has a unique Steiner tree.

Moreover, the set of configurations on which at least two locally minimal trees have the same length also has dimension at most 2n − 1.

The proof relies on the theory of subanalytic sets (Bierstone–Milman) and on the following theorem.

Oblakov’s theorem (2009)

There are no two distinct topologies T1, T2 and a planar configuration P for which the locally minimal trees ST1(P) and ST2(P) are codirected at the terminals, that is, coincide in a small neighborhood of P.

A short proof of Oblakov’s theorem (Cherkashin, 2026) arXiv:2608.22585

The new ingredient is a combinatorial theorem: let a cubic bipartite plane graph be drawn so that at every vertex the three edges are parallel to three fixed pairwise non-parallel lines (one edge per direction). Then the number of “claws” (three edges in one open half-plane) equals the number of “tripods”.

Connectedness of the set of unambiguous configurations

Theorem (Basok–Cherkashin–Rastegaev–Teplitskaya, 2024) arXiv:1809.01463

The set of n-point configurations in ℝd with a unique Steiner tree is path-connected. The proof uses the universal Steiner tree, which contains a unique tree of any given combinatorics.

Theorem (Edelsbrunner–Strelkova, 2012)

For a fixed embedding class, the set of configurations with a unique Steiner tree of that class is path-connected.

Riemannian manifolds

Alternative (Basok–Cherkashin–Rastegaev–Teplitskaya, 2024) arXiv:1809.01463

Replace the plane by an analytic Riemannian manifold M. Then the set of n-point configurations admitting two locally minimal trees of equal length either has Hausdorff dimension strictly less than n·dim M or has non-empty interior. An example of a two-dimensional surface realizing the second option is constructed.

A corollary for minimizers

Proposition (Cherkashin–Teplitskaya, 2024) arXiv:2212.05607

Call a finite M ambiguous if the maximal distance minimizer problem has several solutions for all small enough r. For n ≥ 4 the set of planar n-point ambiguous M has Hausdorff dimension 2n − 1.

Open

Is the Steiner tree unique for a generic configuration in ℝd, d ≥ 3? Does Oblakov’s theorem hold in ℝd? See open questions.

Infinite Steiner trees

The general Steiner problem for compact sets and explicit examples with infinitely many branchings

The general Steiner problem: for a compact set A ⊂ ℝd find a closed set St of minimal length such that St ∪ A is connected. A solution is called a Steiner set, and a Steiner tree if it is connected (for example when A is totally disconnected).

Existence and regularity theorem (Paolini–Stepanov, 2013)

For a compact A a solution exists (also in any proper metric space). If ℋ¹(St) < ∞, then:

  • St ∪ A is compact and St contains no loops;
  • St \ A has at most countably many connected components, each of positive length;
  • the closure of every component is a topological tree with endpoints on A and at most countably many branching points;
  • for almost every ε > 0 the set Stε = St \ Bε(A) is an embedding of a finite graph.
Example: infinite length (Cherkashin–Prozorov–Teplitskaya, 2026) arXiv:2602.11294

There is a countable compact set Abad in the unit square with a single accumulation point such that every Steiner set for it has infinite length. So the assumption ℋ¹(St) < ∞ is necessary.

The universal tree

Let Σ(Λ) be an embedding of the infinite binary tree with root y0 = (0, 0), first descendant y1 = (1, 0), angles 2π/3 at the branchings, and ratio λi between the edge lengths of levels i+1 and i. Let A∞(Λ) be the set of limit points (“leaves”) together with y0; it is uncountable.

Theorem (Paolini–Stepanov–Teplitskaya, 2015) Adv. Calc. Var. 8(3)

If λi < 1/5000 and Σ λi < π/5040, then Σ(Λ) is the unique Steiner tree for A∞(Λ). It is a full planar Steiner tree with infinitely many branching points connecting an uncountable set.

Corollary (universality). Every connected closed subset of Σ(Λ) is the unique Steiner tree for the set of its vertices of degree 1 and 2. Hence Σ(Λ) contains a unique Steiner tree of any finite combinatorics.

Theorem: the self-similar tree (Cherkashin–Teplitskaya, 2023) arXiv:2302.02189

For a constant sequence λi = λ < 1/300 the tree Σ(Λ) is a Steiner tree for A∞. This answers a question of Paolini–Stepanov–Teplitskaya: the terminal set can have positive Hausdorff dimension, equal to ln 2 / ln(1/λ), and the tree itself is a self-similar fractal of length 1/(1 − 2λ).

The proof uses symmetries rather than stability estimates, and does not give uniqueness. Uniqueness for small enough λ was obtained by Paolini and Stepanov (arXiv:2304.01932).

Terminals on the sides of an angle

Consider an angle of size 2α with vertex A∞ and points Ak, Bk on its sides at distance λk−1 from the vertex. Let 𝒜1 = {A∞} ∪ {Ak, Bk}k≥1.

Theorem 1 (Cherkashin–Paolini–Teplitskaya, J. Comb. Optim. 2025) arXiv:2404.11546

Let 0 < α < π/6, 0 < λ ≤ 1/2 and √λ < cos(π/3 + α) / cos(π/3 − α). Then every solution for 𝒜1 is a union of full trees on 5 terminals, that is, it is decomposable. Its length equals

| cos α + √3 sin α + 2λ1 − λ² eπi/6 sin α + 2λ²1 − λ² e−πi/6 sin α |.

Corollary. For the finite set {A1, …, A2k+1, B1, …, B2k} the problem has 2k − 1 different solutions (as stated in the paper), each a union of rescaled and mirrored copies of the tree on {A1, A2, A3, B1, B2}. These solutions resemble the Chung–Graham “ladders”.

Theorem 2 (Cherkashin–Paolini–Teplitskaya, 2025) arXiv:2404.11546

Add the segment [A0B0], where |A0A∞| = |B0A∞| = 1/λ − tan α / (√3 λ), and put 𝒜0 = 𝒜1 ∪ [A0B0]. Under the same assumptions:

  • the problem for 𝒜0 has exactly two solutions, symmetric with respect to the bisector; both are indecomposable trees;
  • the length of the solutions is cos α / λ − sin α / (√3 λ) + √3 sin α / (1 − λ);
  • each solution is self-similar: the homothety with center A∞ and ratio λ² maps it into itself.
Parallelism of locally minimal immersions (Cherkashin–Paolini–Teplitskaya, 2025) arXiv:2404.11546

Two locally minimal immersions of the same (possibly infinite) graph into ℝd with the same fixed vertices have equal length, if it is finite, and corresponding edges are parallel.

See also the tree with four accumulation points of branchings.

Ahlfors–David regularity of Steiner trees

How much tree can fit in a ball without terminals

The Paolini–Stepanov theorem (see infinite trees) says that away from the terminals a Steiner tree is a finite graph, but not how large. The paper by Cherkashin, Prozorov and Teplitskaya gives quantitative bounds depending only on the dimension.

A Steiner tree is called Ahlfors–David regular if there are c, C > 0 such that c ≤ ℋ¹(St ∩ Br(x)) / r ≤ C for every ball centered at x ∈ St that does not intersect A. One can always take c = 2. For a finite A the solution is regular in the usual sense with sharp constants 1 and 3.

Dimension d > 2

In ℝd, d > 2, there is no universal constant C for the whole ball: N random points on the sphere give a tree of arbitrarily large length inside the ball. So one bounds the length in a smaller ball.

Theorem (Cherkashin–Prozorov–Teplitskaya, 2026) arXiv:2602.11294

Let d > 2, let St be a Steiner tree for A with ℋ¹(St) < ∞, let the open ball Bs(x) be disjoint from A, and let ρ ∈ (0, 1). Then

ℋ¹(St ∩ Bρs(x))s ≤ (144d1 − ρ)d−2.

Corollary. St ∩ Bρs(x) consists of at most (144d / (1 − ρ))d−1 segments; the number of branching points in this ball is bounded by the same quantity.

In the Paolini–Stepanov notation. For all ε > 0, ρ ∈ (0, 1), x ∈ Stε: ℋ¹(Stε ∩ Bρε(x)) < ε·(144d / (1 − ρ))d−2.

Points on a sphere (Cherkashin–Prozorov–Teplitskaya, 2026) arXiv:2602.11294

For d > 2 a Steiner tree for any t points on the sphere 𝕊d−1 has length at most cd·t(d−2)/(d−1), where cd = 2 + o(1) and cd ≤ c3 = 11.78… for all d.

Lower bound (Cherkashin–Prozorov–Teplitskaya, 2026) arXiv:2602.11294

For the cube vertices A = {−1/√d, 1/√d}d and 1 ≥ ρ ≥ 1 − 1/(10d) one has ℋ¹(St ∩ Bρ(o)) ≥ 2(2d − 1)√(3d)·(1 − 2√(3d(1 − ρ²))). A large gap remains between the upper and lower bounds.

The plane

Sharp structural results (Cherkashin–Prozorov–Teplitskaya, 2026) arXiv:2602.11294

Let St be a Steiner tree of finite length for A ⊂ ℝ², and let the open disc Br(x) be disjoint from A.

  • The length of St ∩ Br(x) is at most 2πr.
  • The total length of the components of St ∩ Br(x) that have at least one branching point is at most 4πr/3 + r = 5.188…·r.
  • At most two components of St ∩ Br(x) have branching points.
  • A full Steiner tree with at least three terminals on the unit circle has length at least √3.
  • For a full tree on a finite subset of the unit circle, ℋ¹(St) = Σ cos βi, where βi is the angle between the edge at a terminal and the radius.
Four accumulation points (Cherkashin–Prozorov–Teplitskaya, 2026) arXiv:2602.11294

There is a full Steiner tree for a countable terminal set on a circle whose set of branching points has 4 accumulation points. Hence even in the plane the bound on the number of segments fails for ρ = 1.

This number cannot be increased: if A is a totally disconnected subset of a circle and the solution is a full tree, then A has at most 4 accumulation points. As a consequence, in a disc without terminals the set of branching points has at most 8 accumulation points (a bound that does not seem to be tight).

The same arguments carry over word for word to the Gilbert–Steiner problem with p = 0. Questions about sharp constants are in open questions.

Gilbert–Steiner problem

Branched optimal transport with finite supports

Given two measures μ+, μ− of equal mass with finite supports, a flow is a finite graph with masses on the edges that transports μ+ to μ−; vertices outside the supports are called branching points. Moving mass m along a segment of length l costs c(m)·l; the classical case is c(m) = mp for a fixed 0 < p < 1. Concavity of the cost makes merging flows profitable. As p → 0 one recovers the Steiner problem.

Degree of branching points

Theorem (Lippmann–Sanmartín–Hamprecht, 2022)

A solution of the planar Gilbert–Steiner problem has no branching point of degree 5 or more.

Theorem (Cherkashin–Petrov, Pure Appl. Funct. Anal. 2025) arXiv:2309.04202

In a solution of the planar Gilbert–Steiner problem every branching point has degree 3. The statement holds for all “admissible” cost functions of the form c(t) = (∫ sin²(tx) dλ(x))1/2, where λ is a Borel measure with uncountable support and ∫ min(x², 1) dλ < ∞; the case c(t) = tp corresponds to dλ = const·x−2p−1dx.

Theorem (Cherkashin, 2025; to appear in J. Convex Anal.) arXiv:2507.13532

The Gilbert–Steiner problem in ℝd with cost mp admits only triple branchings if and only if d = 2 or p < 1/2.

  • If p ≥ 1/2, there is a solution consisting of d + 1 segments adjacent to one branching point.
  • For p = 1/2 the degree of a branching point in ℝd is at most d + 1.
  • General condition: if c is differentiable and (c²)′ is strictly convex, there are no branchings of degree greater than 3; for k = 3 the three edges are coplanar.
Optimality criterion for a star (Volz–Brazil–Ras–Swanepoel–Thomas, 2013)

For one source q and sinks p1, …, pn with masses mi, the star centered at o is optimal if and only if Σ c(mi)ei + c(Σ mi)e = 0 and |Σi∈I c(mi)ei|² ≤ c²(Σi∈I mi) for all I ⊆ {1, …, n}.

Cherkashin’s question (MDS 2026) arXiv:2611.03403

Find non-trivial upper and lower bounds on the maximal degree of a branching point for large d and fixed p > 1/2. Is there a bound depending only on d? Spherical codes give an upper bound (the angles between edges are bounded from below), but numerically a critical set I may have more than d elements.

Maximal distance minimizers

The shortest connected set whose r-neighborhood covers a given compact set

Let M ⊂ ℝd be compact. Put FM(Σ) = maxy∈M dist(y, Σ).

  • Primal problem. For l > 0 find a connected compact set Σ of length ℋ¹(Σ) ≤ l minimizing FM(Σ).
  • Dual problem. For r > 0 find a connected compact set Σ of minimal length with FM(Σ) ≤ r, that is, M ⊂ Br(Σ).

A solution is called a maximal distance minimizer (an r-minimizer). Interpretation: the shortest gas pipeline coming within distance r of every house. The problem was posed in general form by Buttazzo, Oudet and Stepanov (2002), and in its current form in the papers of Paolini–Stepanov (2004) and Miranda–Paolini–Stepanov (2006).

Basic properties

Theorem (Paolini–Stepanov, 2004; Miranda–Paolini–Stepanov, 2006)
  • A solution exists (Blaschke and Gołąb theorems).
  • A minimizer contains no loops.
  • A minimizer is Ahlfors regular; in the plane the constants are absolute.
  • In the nondegenerate case the primal and dual problems have the same solutions; in particular ℋ¹(Σ) = l and FM(Σ) = r.
Observations (Basok–Cherkashin–Teplitskaya, 2022) arXiv:2212.01903

(i) If Σ is an r-minimizer for some M, then Σ is an r-minimizer for the closure of Br(Σ). (ii) Then Σ is also an r′-minimizer for the closure of Br′(Σ) for all 0 < r′ < r.

A local minimizer is a connected closed set Σ of finite length with FM(Σ) ≤ r that is not longer than any admissible Σ′ differing from it on a set of small diameter. Regularity properties usually hold for local minimizers as well.

Energetic points

A point x ∈ Σ is called energetic if FM(Σ \ Bρ(x)) > FM(Σ) for all ρ > 0. For such a point there is y ∈ M with |xy| = r and Br(y) ∩ Σ = ∅. A minimizer splits into three parts:

Σ = EΣ ⊔ XΣ ⊔ SΣ

— non-isolated energetic points, isolated energetic points, and non-energetic points (the “Steiner part”). Near a non-energetic point the minimizer is a segment or a regular tripod. The set Σ \ Br(M) is a Steiner forest with terminals on ∂Br(M).

The plane versus ℝd (Cherkashin–Teplitskaya, survey 2024) arXiv:2212.05607

In the plane the turn of an arc of a minimizer near an energetic point is less than 2π in absolute value; this is the main tool of planar proofs, and it fails in ℝd, d > 2. Moreover, in ℝd a minimizer may have no non-energetic points at all: if Σ is a minimizer for M ⊂ ℝd, then Σ × {0} is a minimizer for (M × {0}) ∪ (Σ × {r}) ⊂ ℝd+1, and all its points are non-isolated energetic.

Finite M and the link to Steiner trees

A minimizer for a finite M is a union of at most 2·#M − 3 segments: the problem reduces to connecting the r-neighborhoods of the points. If the balls are pairwise disjoint, a minimizer is a Steiner tree for some points on the spheres ∂Br(a), a ∈ M. More in the article on the inverse problem.

Approximation by Steiner trees (Cherkashin–Teplitskaya, 2024) arXiv:2212.05607

Γ-convergence. If compact sets Mi converge to M, then the functionals FMi Γ-converge to FM (on the space of connected compact sets of length ≤ l with the Hausdorff distance). Consequently every limit point of minimizers for Mi is a minimizer for M.

Every minimizer is a limit of finite Steiner trees: Steiner trees Σk for finite 1/k-nets in Σ converge to Σ, and ℋ¹(Σk) → ℋ¹(Σ). Similar results are due to Alvarado, Krishnamoorthy and Vixie (2021).

Complexity

NP-hardness (Cherkashin–Teplitskaya, 2024) arXiv:2212.05607

A discrete version of the problem (integer coordinates, edge lengths rounded up) is NP-complete; hence the original problem is NP-hard. The proof is a reduction to the Garey–Graham–Johnson construction for the Steiner problem.

No ruler-and-compass construction

Already for a generic three-point M in the plane a minimizer cannot be constructed with ruler and compass: for a large angle and a suitable r the problem coincides with Alhazen’s billiard problem (reflection in a spherical mirror), whose unconstructability was shown by Neumann (1998). For Steiner trees such a construction exists (Melzak).

Length bounds

Lower bounds (Basok–Cherkashin–Teplitskaya, 2022) arXiv:2212.01903

For any M ⊂ ℝd the length of an r-minimizer is at least

max (0, ℋd(M) − ωdrdωd−1rd−1),

where ωk is the volume of the unit ball in ℝk. If M is a planar convex set, then ℋ¹(Σ) ≥ (ℋ¹(∂M) − 2πr) / 2.

Penalized form and uniqueness

Penalized form (Cherkashin–Teplitskaya, 2024) arXiv:2212.05607

For any λ > 1, minimizing FM(Σ) + λ(ℋ¹(Σ) − l)+ over connected compact sets is equivalent to the maximal distance minimizer problem. Solutions of the λ-penalized problem FM(S) + λℋ¹(S) → min inherit all regularity properties of minimizers.

Uniqueness (Cherkashin–Teplitskaya, 2024) arXiv:2212.05607

For a circle M = ∂BR and r < R the set of minimizers is uncountable (rotations). If r equals the radius of the smallest ball containing M, the solution is a unique point. For finite M and small r ambiguous configurations are rare: see the corollary in the uniqueness article.

Comparison with average distance minimizers

In the related problem one minimizes the integral ∫ A(dist(y, Σ)) dφ(y) instead of the maximum. Many properties are parallel: absence of loops, Ahlfors regularity, blow-up limits (a radius, a diameter, an angle of at least 2π/3, a regular tripod). In the plane an average distance minimizer is a finite union of simple curves (Buttazzo–Stepanov, 2003), and away from branching and corner points it is locally C1 (Lemenant, 2011).

Regularity of maximal distance minimizers

What a minimizer looks like near each of its points

A ray (ax] is a tangent ray of a set Σ at x if there is a sequence xk ∈ Σ \ {x}, xk → x, with ∠xkxa → 0.

The plane

Theorem (Teplitskaya, 2019) arXiv:1910.07630

Let Σ be a maximal distance minimizer for a compact M ⊂ ℝ² and r > 0. Then:

  • Σ is a union of a finite number of simple curves (injective images of a segment) having one-sided tangents at every point;
  • the angle between any two tangent rays at any point is at least 2π/3;
  • there are at most three tangent rays at a point; if there are three, then in some neighborhood of the point Σ coincides with a regular tripod.

Thus a planar minimizer is isotopic to a finite Steiner tree even for a “bad” compact M. This distinguishes it from a solution of the Steiner problem, which can have infinitely many branching points. The results also hold for local minimizers.

Euclidean space

Theorem (Gordeev–Teplitskaya, Ann. Sc. Norm. Super. Pisa 2025) arXiv:2207.13745

Let Σ be a (local) minimizer for a compact M ⊂ ℝn and r > 0. Then:

  • at every point of Σ there are at most three tangent rays, with pairwise angles at least 2π/3 (so they are coplanar);
  • tangent rays coincide with one-sided tangents and correspond one-to-one to the connected components of Σ \ {x}; in particular the angle between one-sided tangents cannot be zero;
  • a local minimizer contains no loops;
  • for n = 2 the set Σ is a finite union of simple curves with one-sided tangents continuous from the corresponding side.
Sharp Ahlfors constants (Gordeev–Teplitskaya, 2025) arXiv:2207.13745

ℋ¹(Σ ∩ Bε(x)) = ordxΣ · ε + o(ε), where ordxΣ ∈ {1, 2, 3} is the order of the point. In the plane these are the best possible constants; in ℝn the result is “local” (ε depends on x).

Continuity lemma (Gordeev–Teplitskaya, 2025) arXiv:2207.13745

Let Σ ⊂ ℝ² be a (local) minimizer and Σ1 a component of Σ \ {x} with one-sided tangent (ax]. Then for points x̄ ∈ Σ1 any one-sided tangent at x̄ makes an angle o(1) with the line (ax) as x̄ → x.

How smooth the curves can be

Infinitely many corner points (Basok–Cherkashin–Teplitskaya, 2022) arXiv:2212.01903

There is a planar minimizer with infinitely many corner points. So the curves in the regularity theorem need not be C1, and a minimizer cannot be represented as a finite union of smooth curves. The construction is in the article on the inverse problem.

Open for d ≥ 3

Is a minimizer in ℝd a finite union of simple curves? Is there a nonplanar minimizer with infinitely many branching points? Does the continuity lemma hold? See open questions.

Horseshoe conjecture

Minimizers for a circle and for convex curves

Let M be a closed convex curve and Mr the inner part of the boundary of Br(M), that is, the curve parallel to M at distance r inside. A horseshoe is the union of an arc of Mr and two segments tangent to Mr at the ends of the arc.

Conjecture (Miranda–Paolini–Stepanov, 2006)

For a circle of radius R > r every minimizer is a horseshoe. Open for r < R ≤ 4.98r.

Main theorem

Theorem (Cherkashin–Teplitskaya, ESAIM: COCV 2018) arXiv:1511.01026

Let M be a convex closed curve with radius of curvature at least 5r at every point. Then every minimizer for M is a horseshoe. If M is a circle of radius R, the condition R > 4.98r suffices.

Corollary (local minimizers). Let the minimal radius of curvature be R > 5r. If a local minimizer Σ̂ is not a horseshoe, then ℋ¹(Σ̂) − ℋ¹(Σ) ≥ (R − 5r)/2 for any global minimizer Σ.

In the proof, the components of Σ outside the convex hull of Mr turn out to be locally minimal networks on at most 4 points; a case analysis of their combinatorial types and an estimate of the turn of the boundary finish the proof.

Structure for smooth convex curves

Theorem (Cherkashin–Gordeev–Strukov–Teplitskaya, 2020) arXiv:2011.10463

Let M be a convex closed curve with minimal radius of curvature greater than r. Then the closure of every connected component of Br(M) ∩ Σ is a local Steiner tree connecting at most five vertices. The convex hull of such a component is a segment, a triangle or a quadrangle, and it has at most three “entering” points.

“Derivation in the picture” (Cherkashin–Gordeev–Strukov–Teplitskaya, 2020) arXiv:2011.10463

Let y ∈ M, Br(y) ∩ Σ = ∅, and let ∂Br(y) contain energetic points x1, x2. Then (i) they lie on opposite sides of the line through y and the nearest point of Mr; (ii) the derivatives of the length of Σ near x1 and x2, as y moves along M, are equal. The derivative is always nonnegative.

The derivative Γ′(0) is computed in all four cases (d is the degree of the energetic point, k the number of points of M corresponding to it):

dkΓ′(0)

12cos(α + δ) sin(α + β) / sin 2α

Here α, β, γ, δ are angles between the edges of Σ, the segments [xy] and the curve M (precise definitions are in the paper). In addition, an isolated energetic point of degree 1 is collinear with its edge and the point y(x), and at a point of degree 2 the segment [xy(x)] bisects the angle.

The stadium: without a curvature assumption the statement fails

Counterexample (Cherkashin–Teplitskaya, 2018, 2024) arXiv:2212.05607

A stadium is the boundary of the R-neighborhood of a segment; its minimal radius of curvature is R. If R < 1.75r and the stadium is long enough, then no horseshoe is a minimizer: a connected set made of copies of a locally minimal tree Σ0 has length αL + O(1) with α < 2, whereas any horseshoe has length 2L + O(1).

For R > 5r every solution for a stadium is a horseshoe. Any minimizer for a stadium consists of segments and arcs of circles of radius R − r.

Open questions (Teplitskaya, 2025–2026) arXiv:2511.18217
  • Find the minimizers for a circle with r < R < 4.98r.
  • Describe the minimizers for an R-stadium with r < R < 5r.
  • Find the supremum of C for which a Cr-stadium has a minimizer that is not a horseshoe, and the infimum of c for which every minimizer for any curve with minimal radius of curvature cr is a horseshoe.
  • For two parallel segments of length l at distance a > 2r find s(a) = lim ℋ¹(Σ(l))/l. It is known that s(a) = 2 for a > 10r.

Minimizer for a rectangle

An explicit solution for the boundary of a rectangle and small r

Theorem (Cherkashin–Gordeev–Strukov–Teplitskaya, 2021) arXiv:2106.00809

Let M = A1A2A3A4 be a rectangle. There is r0(M) > 0 such that for 0 < r < r0(M) every maximal distance minimizer has the same topology of 21 segments, shown in the figure.

The length of any minimizer is Per(M) − c·r + o(r), where Per(M) is the perimeter and c ≈ 8.473981.

Every minimizer is close, in the Hausdorff distance, to the one depicted.

The solution is surprisingly asymmetric: three sides are followed entirely, while the fourth has a gap. Where the 21 comes from: 4 segments at each vertex (two links of the whisker and two arms of the Steiner point), three segments along sides, and two slanted segments at the side with the gap.

The proof makes heavy use of derivation in the picture for a piecewise smooth curve and of the regularity results.

Remark on narrow rectangles (Cherkashin–Teplitskaya, 2024) arXiv:2212.05607

The condition r < r0(M) matters: for a narrow enough rectangle and some r a minimizer has a different topology (by analogy with the stadium).

Open (Teplitskaya, 2025) arXiv:2511.18217

Find the minimizers for regular n-gons. Presumably for n ≥ 6 it is just a polyline; the question is interesting already for n = 3. Regularity only implies that a solution is a finite union of segments with angles at least 2π/3. For a disc or ball BR nothing is known beyond the general properties.

Inverse problem for minimizers

Which connected sets are minimizers for some M and r

The inverse problem: given a connected compact set Σ, decide whether it is a maximal distance minimizer for some compact M and some r > 0. All results in this article are from the paper of Basok, Cherkashin and Teplitskaya (arXiv:2212.01903).

Steiner trees as minimizers

Theorem (Basok–Cherkashin–Teplitskaya, 2022) arXiv:2212.01903

Let St be a Steiner tree for terminals A = (a1, …, an) in ℝd, and suppose that for some r > 0 every Steiner tree for every n-tuple in the closed 2r-neighborhood of A (in the Chebyshev distance) has the same topology. Then St is an r-minimizer for some n-point set M.

In particular, a Steiner tree that is unique for its terminals is a minimizer for some n-point M and small enough r.

Proposition (Basok–Cherkashin–Teplitskaya, 2022) arXiv:2212.01903

Let St be a full Steiner tree for a1, …, an ∈ ℝd, and let q be the smallest distance from a terminal to its adjacent branching point. If r < q, then St \ ∪Br(ai) is an r-minimizer for M = {a1, …, an}. (This is exactly the figure in the article on minimizers.)

Corollary (a converse in the plane) (Basok–Cherkashin–Teplitskaya, 2022) arXiv:2212.01903

If St is a full Steiner tree for a1, …, an ∈ ℝ² and it is not unique, then St is not a minimizer for any n-point set M. The simplest example is the tree for the vertices of a square (n = 4). The proof relies on Oblakov’s theorem.

Convexity argument. Fix a topology and choose one point in each ball Br(m), connecting them according to this topology. The length is a convex function on a convex set, so local minima are global. The proofs of the theorems in this section rest on this.

A polyline with infinitely many corners

Theorem (Basok–Cherkashin–Teplitskaya, 2022) arXiv:2212.01903

Let points a1, a2, … lie on a circle ∂BR(o), with N·|a1a2| = r for a large enough N, |ai+1ai+2| = |aiai+1|/2, let a∞ be the limit point, and let a∞+1 lie on the tangent line at a∞ at distance r/N. Then the polyline Σ = ∪[aiai+1] is the unique minimizer for the compact set M = {vi}, where vi is the point at distance r from ai with ∠ai−1aivi = ∠ai+1aivi.

This minimizer has infinitely many corner points, that is, points without a tangent line.

Smooth curves

Theorem (Basok–Cherkashin–Teplitskaya, 2022) arXiv:2212.01903

Every simple C1,1-curve γ ⊂ ℝd is a maximal distance minimizer for M = Br(γ) and small enough r. The reason: for such a curve the volume lower bound on the length (see length bounds) is sharp.

Tilli’s theorem generalized to ℝd (Basok–Cherkashin–Teplitskaya, 2022) arXiv:2212.01903

For a rectifiable curve γ ⊂ ℝd and R > 0 the following are equivalent: (i) ℋd(BR(γ)) = ℋ¹(γ)ωd−1Rd−1 + ωdRd; (ii) every point of BR(γ) has a unique nearest point on γ; (iii) the same holds and the radius of curvature of γ is at least R.

A curve satisfying (ii) (for example, of length at most πR with radius of curvature at least R, or any simple C1,1-curve for small R) is an average distance minimizer for Ω = BR(γ). The planar case is Tilli’s theorem (2010).

Open

Let Σ be an r-minimizer for some M. Is it the unique r-minimizer for the closure of Br(Σ)? Does the set of energetic points determine the minimizer? Is there an analogue of the converse statement for d > 2 and for non-full trees?

Open questions

From the surveys of Cherkashin–Teplitskaya (2024), Teplitskaya (2025) and the MDS 2026 problem collection

Sources: arXiv:2212.05607, arXiv:2511.18217 (21 questions), arXiv:2611.03403, arXiv:2602.11294.

Minimizers: explicit examples

Circle and stadium

Minimizers for a circle with r < R < 4.98r; for an R-stadium with r < R < 5r; the sharp threshold constants. See the horseshoe conjecture.

Any explicit set

A disc or ball BR ⊂ ℝd (nothing is known beyond general properties); regular n-gons (already n = 3); the rectangle for r that is not small.

Minimizers: regularity and uniqueness

Finiteness in ℝd

Is a minimizer in ℝd, d ≥ 3, a union of finitely many injective curves? Is there a nonplanar minimizer with infinitely many branching points?

Universal Ahlfors regularity

Are there constants C1, C2 depending only on d such that C1ε ≤ ℋ¹(Σ ∩ Bε(x)) ≤ C2ε for all small ε?

Nonplanar branching

Construct a minimizer with a branching point x no neighborhood of which in Σ lies in a plane. Does the continuity lemma for one-sided tangents hold in ℝd?

Uniqueness for the own neighborhood

If Σ is an r-minimizer for some M, is it the unique r-minimizer for the closure of Br(Σ)? Does the set GΣ of energetic points determine the minimizer?

Ambiguous configurations in ℝd

Find the Hausdorff dimension of the set of n-point ambiguous M ⊂ ℝd for d ≥ 3, n ≥ 4; at least prove that it has measure zero.

Algebraic complexity

For a finite M ⊂ ℝ² of n points with rational coordinates: what is the largest degree of the extension of ℚ generated by the energetic points? Open already for n = 4. (For three points Alhazen’s problem arises, a quartic equation.)

Steiner trees as minimizers

Let a totally disconnected compact A ⊂ ℝd have at least two Steiner trees S and T. Can S be a maximal distance minimizer? For d = 2 the answer is negative if S or T is full.

Steiner trees

Oblakov’s theorem in ℝd

Is there a finite set of terminals in ℝd, d ≥ 3, with two different Steiner trees that coincide near the terminals?

Uniqueness and existence

Is the solution unique for a generic input (beyond the finite planar case)? Is there a bounded non-closed A ⊂ ℝd for which the problem has no solution (a question of E. Paolini)?

Steiner ratio

The Gilbert–Pollak conjecture in the plane (Graham’s $1000 prize), the value in ℝ³ ($500), and higher dimensions.

Density in a ball without terminals (questions of G. David)

What is the largest number of points of ∂Btr(x) ∩ St and the largest number of branchings in Btr(x) for t ∈ (0, 1)? For t = 1/2 in the plane M. Khristoforov gave an example with 6 points; it is unknown whether this is the maximum. How do the bounds (144d/(1 − ρ))d−2 and (144d/(1 − ρ))d−1 from the regularity theorem behave in d and 1 − ρ?

Random and extremal sets

Does the length of a Steiner tree for N random points have asymptotics βN(d−1)/d? Can the constant be separated from those for the spanning tree, the rectilinear Steiner problem and the travelling salesman? Let LΩ(n) be the largest length of a Steiner tree for n points of a planar domain Ω; the hexagonal lattice gives lim LΩ(n)/√(n·ℋ²(Ω)) ≥ (4/3)1/4 = 1.07… Is it true that n points in a rectangle √3 × (n − 1) can always be connected by a set of length at most √3(n − 1) + o(1)?

Number of simultaneously optimal topologies

How many full topologies can give a shortest solution simultaneously? In all known examples at most exponentially many in n, while the number of topologies grows factorially.

Nonplanar examples

Describe the Steiner tree for the union of homothetic copies of the vertices of a regular n-gon in ℝ³. Construct a nonplanar unique full Steiner tree with infinitely many branchings. Is the self-similar binary tree unique whenever the ratios are uniformly bounded?

Gilbert–Steiner

Bounds on the branching degree for large d and p > 1/2: see the Gilbert–Steiner problem. Quantitative regularity of branched transport.
